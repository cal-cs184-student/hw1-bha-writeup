Overview

What I built

  • I built a vector graphics renderer that turns simplified SVG files into raster images. It drew single-color triangles, antialiased them with supersampling, placed drawings with hierarchical transforms, and mapped textures with pixel and mipmap level sampling.
  • The pipeline flowed from SVGParser to SVGElement scaled draws to my RasterizerImp functions in src/rasterizer.cpp, with transforms in src/transforms.cpp and texture sampling in src/texture.cpp.

My approach per task

  • Task 1: I rasterized triangles by testing pixel centers against three edge functions inside a clamped bounding box, with winding normalization and the top-left edge rule.
  • Task 2: I widened the sample buffer to hold a grid of subsamples per pixel and resolved them by averaging, keeping points and lines solid through fill_pixel.
  • Task 3: I implemented translate, scale, and counterclockwise rotate as 3x3 homogeneous matrices, then posed cubeman as Superman purely by editing transform attributes.
  • Task 4: I computed barycentric weights from the same edge functions and blended vertex colors per subsample.
  • Task 5: I interpolated texture coordinates with barycentrics and reconstructed texels with nearest or bilinear sampling at full resolution.
  • Task 6: I measured each pixel's texture footprint with uv differentials and selected or blended mipmap levels with nearest or linear filtering.

Problems and fixes

  • Task 4: a dashed white line appeared along one color-wheel spoke at rate 1. I traced it to float rounding flipping shared-edge ownership and added a small epsilon (edge_eps = 0.005) that snaps near-zero edge values into the top-left tie-break, picked as the smallest value clearing the artifact.
  • Task 5: texmap renders differed from references on high-frequency detail. I measured local texture variance and confirmed differing pixels sit on 10x higher variance detail, which is inherent minification noise between implementations rather than a convention bug, and unit tests on known textures all passed.
  • Task 6: the starter generate_mips leaves its smallest level unfilled. Since it sits outside the task modify list and that level was unreachable in practice, I left it alone.
  • General: the starter texture.cpp missed #include <cstdint> for its uint8_t helpers and failed to compile on my toolchain, so I added that single include.

What I learned

  • Everything in this homework boiled down to sampling, meaning the renderer never saw continuous geometry directly and only ever evaluated it at discrete points. Triangles tested pixel centers, supersampling averaged grids of subsamples per pixel, barycentrics weighted the three corners at each sample, and mipmaps matched each pixel to the prefiltered texture level covering its footprint.
  • I was surprised that changing such small values could produce such a large change in output. For example, the edge tolerance defines a tie-break band around each triangle edge, so setting it to 0.005 decided whether boundary samples counted as covered. Likewise, level selection computes a continuous value but must round to a discrete pyramid level, and each level step halves or doubles the texture resolution. I checked each such choice against references, since a constant I could easily overlook ended up redrawing visible regions of the image.
  • Documenting my work allowed me to understand the concepts better. Explaining the magenta gap meant comparing the triangle width against the sample spacing, where a sample spacing wider than the triangle leaves whole rows of pixel centers outside all three edge tests. Stating those two numbers made the dropout mechanism obvious.

Task 1: Drawing Single-Color Triangles

How I rasterize triangles

  • The work happened in RasterizerImp::rasterize_triangle() in src/rasterizer.cpp.
  • Following Lecture 2, I treated rasterization as sampling a 2D indicator function f(x,y) = inside(tri,x,y), so each output pixel was Image[x][y] = f(x + 0.5, y + 0.5). That meant I tested pixel centers, never corners.
  • The point in triangle test was three line tests. A sample was inside only when it passed all three edge functions, that is L0 > 0 && L1 > 0 && L2 > 0.
  • I normalized the winding order first. I computed the signed area and swapped vertices when the triangle was clockwise, so the same test worked for clockwise and counterclockwise input. I checked this with svg/basic/test6.svg.
  • I computed the bounding box of the triangle and clamped it to the framebuffer size.
  • For each pixel in that box, I evaluated the three edge functions at the pixel center.
  • As the lecture notes, the real test needs boundary handling based on edge rules. I used the encouraged OpenGL and Direct3D rule, where a sample exactly on an edge counts as covered when the edge is a top edge or a left edge. A top edge is horizontal and above the triangle. A left edge is not horizontal and sits on the left side of the triangle.
  • When a sample passed, I called the fill_pixel() helper once. This task used one sample per pixel, and supersampling came later in Task 2.

Bounding box efficiency

  • The bounding box is the smallest rectangle that contains the whole triangle.
  • My loops visited exactly the pixels in that clamped box, which was (xmax - xmin + 1) by (ymax - ymin + 1) samples.
  • I never tested pixels outside the box, and I never scanned the full framebuffer.
  • The work per sample is constant, just three edge function evaluations plus the edge rule checks.
  • So the running time grows with the bounding box area, which matches a baseline that checks each sample in the box.

Task 2: Antialiasing by Supersampling

Algorithm and data structures

  • I stored one color per subsample in sample_buffer, sized width * height * sample_rate and indexed as sample_buffer[(y * width + x) * sample_rate + k].
  • I arranged the subsamples as a grid of sqrt(rate) by sqrt(rate) spread evenly over each pixel, at positions x + (i + 0.5) / n and y + (j + 0.5) / n.
  • I kept the Task 1 bounding box, winding normalization, and top-left edge rule in rasterize_triangle(), and I tested every subsample against the three edge functions, writing only the covered ones.

Why supersampling helps

  • A single center sample made edge pixels all or nothing, which caused jaggies and made skinny geometry like the magenta sliver drop out completely.
  • With several samples per pixel, an edge pixel got a mix of covered and uncovered subsamples, so the resolved color was a blend. Edges looked smoother and thin triangles survived instead of vanishing.

Pipeline changes

  • I updated set_sample_rate() and set_framebuffer_target() in src/rasterizer.cpp to resize the buffer to width * height * sample_rate, so it stayed correct when the rate changed with the - and = keys and when the window was resized.
  • I cleared the whole supersample buffer to white in clear_buffers() before each redraw.
  • I changed fill_pixel() to write the same color to all subsamples of a pixel, which kept points and lines working, for example the black border in test4.svg, without needing their own antialiasing.
  • I averaged the subsamples of each pixel in resolve_to_framebuffer() and converted the float color to 8-bit RGB for the display.

Antialiasing my triangles

  • Covered subsamples got the triangle color and uncovered ones stayed white, then the average became the pixel color. A pixel that was half covered rendered as half blend, so staircase edges turned into smooth ramps.

Screenshots

  • At rate 1 the sliver showed a hard gap where whole pixel centers missed the triangle. At rates 4 and 16 the gap filled in with blended pink pixels. This was observed because partially covered pixels then averaged their covered and uncovered subsamples instead of dropping out completely.

Task 3: Transforms

Implementation

  • I filled in translate(dx, dy), scale(sx, sy), and rotate(deg) in src/transforms.cpp following the SVG spec. Each returned a 3x3 matrix in homogeneous coordinates, matching the 9-argument row-major Matrix3x3 constructor.
  • translate(dx, dy) returned rows [1 0 dx], [0 1 dy], [0 0 1].
  • scale(sx, sy) returned rows [sx 0 0], [0 sy 0], [0 0 1].
  • rotate(deg) converted degrees counterclockwise to radians and returned rows [cos -sin 0], [sin cos 0], [0 0 1].

Task 4: Barycentric coordinates

Barycentric coordinates

  • Barycentric coordinates describe a point inside a triangle as a mix of the three corners. I computed three weights called alpha, beta, and gamma, one per vertex, that always add up to 1.
  • Each weight equals the area of the small triangle formed by the sample point and the opposite edge, divided by the area of the whole triangle. I got both areas from the same edge functions I already used for the inside test, so if alpha was the weight for corner 0, it was the edge value of edge 1-2 at the sample divided by that edge value at corner 0.
  • A weight of 1 means the sample sits exactly on that corner, and equal thirds means it sits at the center. Anything outside the triangle gives a negative weight, which is another way to run the inside test.
  • I filled in rasterize_interpolated_color_triangle() in src/rasterizer.cpp by reusing my Task 1 and 2 traversal. That gave me winding normalization with matching color swaps, a clamped bounding box, the top-left edge rule, and the sqrt(rate) by sqrt(rate) supersample grid. I skipped zero area triangles to avoid dividing by zero.
  • For each covered subsample I computed alpha, beta, and gamma as above and set the color to alpha * c0 + beta * c1 + gamma * c2. Since each subsample got its own blended color, the resolve step averaged them with no extra work.

Task 5: "Pixel sampling" for texture mapping

Pixel sampling

  • When I mapped a texture onto a triangle, each screen sample got a texture coordinate (u, v) from barycentric interpolation. Since that coordinate almost never landed exactly on a texel center (a texel is just one pixel of the texture image), I had to reconstruct a single color from the surrounding texels, and that choice is what pixel sampling was about.
  • With nearest sampling I simply returned the closest texel. It cost one texel fetch and kept things sharp, but when the texture was minified it aliased, because neighboring samples could land on completely unrelated texels.
  • With bilinear sampling I blended the four surrounding texels instead, interpolating horizontally first and then vertically. It cost four fetches, but the reconstruction was continuous, so the minification aliasing largely went away.
  • For Texture::sample_nearest() in src/texture.cpp, I converted with tx = floor(u * w) and ty = floor(v * h), clamped into range, and returned that texel.
  • For Texture::sample_bilinear() in src/texture.cpp, I located the sample at x = u * w - 0.5 and y = v * h - 0.5 in texel-center space, took the four neighbors with clamped indices, blended horizontally with the fractional part, then blended those two results vertically.
  • I filled in rasterize_textured_triangle() in src/rasterizer.cpp by reusing my Task 1, 2, and 4 traversal. That gave me winding normalization with matching uv swaps, a clamped bounding box, the top-left edge rule with epsilon, and the supersample grid. For each covered subsample I interpolated the uv with barycentrics and called nearest or bilinear based on psm, always at mipmap level 0 for now.

Screenshots

  • Figures 10 through 13 show svg/texmap/test1.svg with default viewing parameters, all captured with the S hotkey and the pixel inspector over the West Africa coastline.
  • Nearest at 1 sample per pixel showed chunky texels with visible steps along the coast. Nearest at 16 kept that texel chunkiness but smoothed out the triangle coverage edges around it through supersampling.
  • Bilinear at 1 already blended the texels into smooth gradients, a clear step up from nearest at 1. Bilinear at 16 combined texture filtering with coverage antialiasing, so it looked the cleanest overall.
  • The methods diverged most on magnified high-frequency detail like this coastline, where one screen pixel spanned several texels. Nearest committed to a single texel, bilinear filtered four, and supersampling integrated whole-pixel coverage. On uniform regions like the open ocean, all four looked the same.

Task 6: "Level Sampling" with mipmaps for texture mapping

Level sampling

  • Pixel sampling picked texels from one image, but when a triangle was minified, one screen pixel could cover hundreds of texels, and no single-level filter stayed stable. Mipmapping prebuilt a pyramid of downsampled textures, and level sampling picked which pyramid level matched the on-screen footprint of each pixel.
  • I measured the footprint with uv differentials, which track how much the texture coordinate moved when I stepped one pixel right (p_dx_uv - p_uv) and one pixel down (p_dy_uv - p_uv), scaled up by the full texture size into texels. The mipmap level was log2 of the longer of those two steps, so a pixel spanning 4 texels sampled from 2 levels down.
  • I computed the uv at the sample plus the uv at its +1x and +1y neighbors with barycentrics, which gave the differentials their correct perspective behavior across the triangle for free.
  • For Texture::get_level() in src/texture.cpp, I differenced the SampleParams uvs, scaled by the full resolution width and height, took the longer Euclidean length, and returned log2 of it clamped to the available levels.
  • For Texture::sample() in src/texture.cpp, L_ZERO sampled level 0 as in Task 5, L_NEAREST rounded the level and sampled it, and L_LINEAR blended the two adjacent levels by the fractional part. Each level fetch honored psm, so linear pixel sampling on linear level sampling gave trilinear filtering. All level indices clamped into the pyramid.
  • I extended rasterize_textured_triangle() in src/rasterizer.cpp to fill the full SampleParams per covered subsample. That meant p_uv at the subsample plus p_dx_uv and p_dy_uv from barycentrics evaluated one pixel over in x and y. I skipped the extra evaluations in L_ZERO mode and called tex.sample(sp) for every subsample.

Tradeoffs

  • Supersampling brute-forced every edge by shading multiple samples per pixel. It antialiased geometry coverage but multiplied shading cost and sample memory by the rate, and it did nothing for texture detail inside a fully covered pixel.
  • Pixel sampling (nearest vs bilinear) was cheap in memory with no extra storage, and bilinear smoothed magnified texels nicely, but neither helped minification, since one pixel still committed to one texel neighborhood while its footprint covered many.
  • Level sampling added about a third more texture memory for the pyramid, and each fetch stayed constant cost (one or two level reads). It was the only one of the three that stayed stable under heavy minification, trading a controlled amount of blur for the shimmer it removed.
